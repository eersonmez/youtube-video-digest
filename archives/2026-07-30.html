The discussion examines how organizations can secure AI-generated code, coding agents, and software supply chains.

Scott Hanselman · 2026-07-30T16:31:52+00:00

Varun Badhwar

Scott Hanselman interviews Varun Badhwar, founder and CEO of Endor Labs, about securing software as AI agents and citizen developers dramatically increase code and dependency production. Badhwar says developer workstations are now prime targets because they contain cloud, Git, package-manager, and other credentials, while malicious packages or agent skills can steal secrets before conventional pull-request or CI/CD checks run. He recommends zero-trust development, stronger workstation controls, mature CI/CD, and moving security checks into the coding-agent generation loop. Badhwar cites Endor Labs benchmarks indicating that 70% to 80% of AI-recommended code is insecure by default, while arguing that LLMs alone are too costly, slow, and nondeterministic for dependable security. Combining deterministic static analysis with security-focused data and LLM reasoning can provide contextual remediation much faster. The broader challenge is clearing accumulated vulnerability debt and automating remediation so humans remain the final verification layer.

  • AI agents and citizen developers are increasing code and dependency volume.
  • Developer workstations are high-value targets because they expose credentials and privileged tools.
  • Malicious packages and agent skills can enable rapid secret theft before CI/CD checks.
  • Security should move into coding-agent generation, pull requests, and the broader SDLC.
  • Static analysis combined with LLM reasoning is faster and more dependable than LLM-only scanning.
  • Zero-trust development and automated remediation are needed for rapidly exploited vulnerabilities.

Essentials: How to Become Resilient, Forge Your Identity & Lead Others | Jocko Willink

In this Huberman Lab Essentials conversation, Andrew Huberman and Jocko Willink discuss discipline, resilience, identity, leadership, and action under adversity. Willink says military performance taught him that doing tasks well can earn greater autonomy and improve future opportunities. He relies on routines rather than fluctuating motivation and says ordinary exercise usually increases energy, with brief high-intensity anaerobic work especially useful for readiness. He describes losses as requiring mourning, analysis, and renewed action, while leaders should counter a team's emotional swings without suppressing appropriate celebration. Willink also explains detachment: stepping back, widening perspective, listening, and separating from personal emotion so better decisions become possible. He applies this in combat, business, and relationships, including recognizing when someone needs to vent rather than receive solutions. He recommends deliberately practicing discomfort to build resilience. The discussion is educational and not personal medical advice.

  • Discipline, rather than motivation, drives daily action.
  • Exercise can increase energy, especially brief high-intensity anaerobic work.
  • After losses, mourn or analyze briefly, then take constructive action.
  • Leaders should detach from group emotion and provide a balancing influence.
  • Stepping back, widening perspective, listening, and practicing discomfort can train resilience.
