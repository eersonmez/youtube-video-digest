How AI's Top New Models Transform Cybersecurity (And Where They Don't) — With Snehal Antani

Alex Kantrowitz interviews Snehal Antani, CEO of Horizon3, about the gap between AI cybersecurity hype and operational reality. Antani says frontier models perform well in labs but struggle in changing networks that actively defend themselves, especially when deception is present. In Horizon3 research, models clicked on decoys 92% of the time, rising to 95–100% when decoys were well placed, compared with 37% for expert human hackers. He identifies patch-diff analysis as a genuine threat because attackers can reverse-engineer and weaponize flaws faster than companies apply fixes. Agents also accelerate reconnaissance and exploit development, expand risks through insecure vibe-coded applications, and behave like privileged insider threats. Prompt injection can harm or expose agents, but defenders can use reverse prompt injection and honey tokens against attackers. Antani argues that detection, containment, eradication, rapid patching, and realistic training remain essential.

  • Frontier models are capable in labs but perform poorly against active defenses and deception.
  • AI agents clicked on decoys 92% of the time, versus 37% for expert human hackers.
  • AI-assisted patch analysis can accelerate vulnerability reverse engineering and exploitation.
  • Vibe-coded applications and overprivileged agents are expanding enterprise attack surfaces.
  • Prompt injection can be used offensively by attackers and defensively through deception.
  • Strong fundamentals remain necessary: detection, containment, eradication, patching, and training.

Fine-grained authorization for securing agentic AI systems and retrieval-augmented generation applications.

NDC Conferences · 2026-07-29T11:00:23+00:00

Fine-Grained Authorization: The Missing Piece in Agentic AI Security - Shivay Lamba - NDC Sydney

Shivay Lamba explains why agentic AI systems need runtime authorization rather than relying only on prompts or roles. He contrasts role-based access control (RBAC) and attribute-based access control (ABAC) with relationship-based access control (ReBAC), which models users, folders, documents, and permissions as a graph and can inherit access through relationships. OpenFGA, an open-source ReBAC system inspired by Google's Zanzibar paper, is presented as one implementation. In a RAG knowledge assistant, Lamba compares post-filtering retrieved results with pre-filtering vector searches using permission metadata; he recommends pre-filtering because unauthorized documents are never returned to the model. A demo shows unrestricted retrieval exposing financial or technical information, while OpenFGA limits results by user role. He also demonstrates task-based authorization for agent tool calls, including Slack actions that may require explicit user approval and limited scope.

  • Agentic systems need guardrails and runtime permissions to prevent unrestricted decisions.
  • ReBAC models subjects, objects, and relationships for detailed folder, document, and action permissions.
  • OpenFGA uses authorization models and relationship tuples to infer inherited access.
  • Pre-filtering vector-search results is safer than filtering documents after retrieval.
  • Task-based authorization can restrict tool calls and require scoped approval for Slack actions.
