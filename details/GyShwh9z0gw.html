The environmental impact of AI data centers regarding water and power consumption and potential solutions.

Alex Kantrowitz · 2026-08-03T15:00:26+00:00 · How Much Water AI Data Centers Actually Use. And What Can Be Done About It. — With Christophe Beck

What This Video Is About

This video addresses the significant environmental impact of the rapidly expanding Artificial Intelligence (AI) data center industry, specifically concerning its demands for water and power resources. The central concern is that the exponential growth of AI infrastructure poses a substantial risk to natural resources; estimates suggest that by 2030, AI could require the annual incremental power equivalent to India and the drinking water volume of the entire United States [1:17-1:29]. Currently, this strain on local resources has led to delays in many data center projects or community opposition.

The discussion pivots from identifying these environmental pressures to exploring viable technological solutions. The primary focus is on moving away from traditional cooling methods that rely heavily on water consumption toward innovative, closed-loop systems. These new approaches aim not only to drastically reduce water usage—potentially making a facility use less water than a car wash—but also to repurpose the massive amounts of waste heat generated by these powerful computing centers for other beneficial uses like district heating or electricity generation.

Detailed Walkthrough

The conversation begins by framing AI as a revolutionary technology comparable to the invention of electricity, but one that requires immense natural resources [0:49-1:05]. The speaker notes the severe resource demands, stating that by 2030, AI will require the incremental power of India and the drinking water of the US annually [1:17-1:29]. This scarcity is evident globally, leading to a situation where 60% of data center projects are delayed due to lack of resources or community pushback [2:02].

The speaker then introduces solutions from Eolab, emphasizing a philosophy aligned with nature's closed cycles. Traditional cooling involves using large AC units and cooling towers that consume significant water by circulating it through the air [5:11-7:38]. The proposed solution is "direct to chip cooling technologies," which deliver a water-based product directly onto each chip via small radiators in a closed loop, allowing for continuous recirculation without needing incremental fresh water [5:27-5:44]. This method allows data centers to operate using less water than a car wash [5:51-5:59].

The technical necessity of this cooling is explained by the relationship between compute performance and heat generation. More powerful chips ingest more data, requiring more power, which in turn generates proportionally more heat that must be removed to prevent chip failure [6:21-7:00]. The shift from air conditioning to direct liquid cooling involves replacing large room AC systems with a system where thin tubes bring the coolant close to each chip. This water is then returned to a central distribution unit for reprocessing, temperature regulation, and purification before being cycled back onto the chips [11:49-12:50].

A critical aspect of this closed-loop system is water purity. The water used to cool chips must be many times purer than drinking water because any impurity can cause biological contamination, scaling, or corrosion when reused at operating temperatures (around 30 Celsius for the chip) [13:06-14:29]. Eolab utilizes technology developed for ultra-pure applications, which is described as being thousands to millions of times purer than drug-grade water, ensuring optimal cooling performance and preventing system failure [14:13-15:55].

Regarding the waste heat generated—which can be equivalent to one nuclear plant per gigawatt of compute power—there are several options for repurposing it. One option is releasing it into the atmosphere; another, particularly in urban settings, is using it for district heating or cooling homes [16:09-17:28]. A third, and a focus of current work, is reusing this heat to generate electricity, effectively reducing the power needed to run the data center itself by converting 40% of the cooling energy into usable power [17:36-18:51].

The industry's growth trajectory is described as exponential. Before COVID-19, roughly one data center was built per year; now, it is one per week, with 50% located in the US and 30% in China [20:09-20:32]. Furthermore, newer generations of chips are being deployed into older data centers every six to ten months, necessitating retrofitting to manage escalating power and water needs [21:07-21:45].

The discussion concludes by contrasting two potential futures. The "bad way" involves continuing the current path—investing billions in infrastructure that is delayed due to resource scarcity or community resistance, leading to environmental degradation like drying out places and increased wildfires [38:01-38:56]. The "good way" involves building infrastructure that reuses water indefinitely, utilizes high-performing chips requiring less power, and locates data centers where they benefit both the environment and local communities [39:43-40:33].

Key Takeaways

  • AI data center expansion is projected to require massive resources, potentially demanding the annual incremental power of India and the drinking water of the US by 2030.
  • Traditional cooling methods using large AC units and cooling towers are highly water-intensive and unsustainable for future growth.
  • Direct-to-chip cooling technologies circulate a water-based product directly onto chips in a closed loop, eliminating the need for incremental fresh water usage.
  • The heat generated by data centers (equivalent to one nuclear plant per gigawatt of compute power) can be repurposed for district heating or electricity generation.
  • Water used in these systems must meet extremely high purity standards—many times purer than drinking water—to prevent scaling, corrosion, and biological contamination during reuse.
  • The industry is currently dominated by air-cooled data centers (around 97% of existing ones), but newer facilities are adopting liquid cooling solutions.
  • AI demand is growing exponentially; the rate of new chip generations means older data centers must be retrofitted to avoid exploding resource needs.
  • A key trade-off exists between maximizing immediate compute performance and ensuring long-term environmental sustainability, with the speaker suggesting cooler chips will prevail.

Practical Notes

  • Technology: Direct to chip cooling technologies utilize thin tubes bringing liquid coolant directly to each chip via small radiators.
  • Workflow (Closed Loop): Coolant is brought to the chip -> cools the chip -> returns to a central distribution unit -> reprocessed for temperature and purity -> cycled back to the chip.
  • Water Purity Requirement: The water must be managed to remove all biocontamination risks, scaling potential, and corrosion agents before reuse, requiring standards far exceeding those of pharmaceutical or drinking water production.
  • Heat Reuse Options: 1) Release into atmosphere (no water use); 2) Use for district heating/cooling in cities; 3) Convert heat to electricity via thermal systems similar to geothermal power generation.
  • Power Consumption Breakdown: Currently, approximately 40% of a data center's power is used for cooling, while 60% is used for computation. Reducing the cooling portion (e.g., from 40% to 10%) increases compute efficiency and chip performance.
