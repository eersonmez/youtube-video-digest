What This Video Is About

The conversation presents Legora as a legal AI company serving law firms and large in-house legal teams and corporations around the world. Jonathan Williams, Legora’s Head of France, describes OpenAI as one of the company’s strongest partners and as a particularly prominent model provider in the legal AI industry. Legora has used every OpenAI model released from GPT-4 onward in different parts of its platform for different tasks.

The discussion also focuses on the speed and uncertainty of AI development. Williams says each new model release generates significant excitement inside the team, comparing the experience to Christmas. Looking ahead six months, he is most excited by the fact that the outcome is difficult to predict, while expecting progress to be both substantial and rapid.

For large, established enterprises, however, adopting AI is not simply a matter of selecting a new model or product. Williams describes transformation as a “bumpy ride” involving resource allocation, retooling, process changes, organizational inertia, and other constraints. His central advice is for organizations to define the future they want, assess the future they can realistically reach, and close the gap between those two points as quickly as possible.

Practical Notes

An organization applying Williams’s advice could begin by writing down its desired future state for AI-enabled legal work. The transcript supports defining this future explicitly, but it does not prescribe what that future should contain. The next step is to assess what is realistically achievable given the organization’s size, resources, existing processes, and constraints.

The gap analysis should account for the issues Williams names directly: available resources, required retooling, process changes, organizational inertia, and the number of moving parts. These factors are especially important in companies with approximately 80,000 to 200,000 employees, where changes may need to pass through many existing structures.

The main risks identified are uncertainty and organizational friction. AI capabilities may change rapidly, while enterprise processes and resource decisions may move slowly.
