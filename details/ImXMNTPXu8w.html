A talk by Clarissa Rodrigues about costly UX and product design mistakes and how evidence-based practices can prevent them.

NDC Conferences · 2026-07-27T09:58:27+00:00 · Most Expensive Design Mistakes (Ever) and how to avoid them - Clarissa Rodrigues

What This Video Is About

Clarissa Rodrigues, a Brazilian designer who works at Uber, argues that expensive design failures usually begin when teams substitute personal opinions for evidence. She describes an earlier stage of her career in which teams assumed that “everyone is a designer,” believed they already knew what customers wanted, and treated UX research as unnecessary. When users failed to use the resulting product as expected, the team had to confront the difference between an interface that looks good to its creators and one that actually works for users.

The talk connects everyday interface decisions to serious consequences. Poor hierarchy can make people abandon a website. Inconsistent labels can make users unsure which action to take. App-only or code-only services can exclude people when they lack a device, battery, connectivity, or technical familiarity. Deceptive controls can lead to accidental subscriptions or financial losses, while unclear vehicle controls can contribute to safety-critical confusion.

Rodrigues presents evidence-based design as a combination of clear visual and interaction patterns, research, experimentation, and behavioral data. Her recommendations include designing for scanning rather than full reading, reusing familiar mental models, maintaining consistent terminology, giving users control and recovery paths, reducing choice overload, and showing system status. Uber’s city-level data and A/B experiments illustrate how large-scale evidence can reveal where users struggle, but the underlying process also applies to teams without Uber’s scale. The healthcare and diabetes examples are educational interface-design examples, not personal medical advice.

Detailed Walkthrough

From personal opinion to observed behavior

Rodrigues opens by explaining that the talk is about costly design mistakes in general, not only at Uber [00:13]. She traces the problem to a common product-development attitude: someone with authority decides what the customer wants, the team builds that solution, and UX research is considered unnecessary. The resulting question is often, “Why are users not using our amazing UI?” Her answer is that the team may have optimized for its own assumptions rather than for the users’ expectations and behavior [00:36].

Uber gives her a particularly large evidence base. She describes the company as having extensive usage data and experience with features and experiments at global scale [01:28]. A view of trips around the world during New Year’s 2023 is used to illustrate the volume and geographic spread of the data [01:59]. That scale makes it possible to investigate questions such as why users are not clicking a button that the team expected them to click, rather than treating that failure as a matter of opinion.

The talk’s initial themes are that users scan information rather than reading every word, familiar patterns should not be reinvented without a reason, and clarity should take priority over cleverness [02:26].

Design for scanning, not idealized reading

Rodrigues cites a figure suggesting that more than 16% of visitors abandon some sites within the first seconds, and uses it to emphasize how little time a design may have to communicate its purpose [02:53]. Her broader point is that people do not usually process a page from top to bottom. They scan according to different patterns and look for visual signals that help them decide where to focus.

She describes herself as a “layer cake” scanner: she looks first at bold text and the most important information, rather than reading every paragraph [03:33]. A useful interface therefore needs to support scanning through headings, emphasis, short sections, images, and clear grouping. She refers to heat maps and eye-tracking examples, including one involving Yamaha, to show how people’s attention is distributed across a page [04:11]. The design implication is not that every user scans identically, but that pages should provide multiple obvious entry points into the content.

This leads to visual hierarchy. Rodrigues highlights size, contrast, proximity, spacing, and alignment as factors that determine whether a page feels easy or difficult to read [04:55]. She compares a newspaper-like presentation with a more web-oriented layout and says that a New York Times redesign increased the number of readers by making the content easier to scan. Her argument is that web content should not simply reproduce the visual structure of a printed page; it should use the strengths of the medium to support quick navigation and selective attention.

Make the next action obvious

Rodrigues applies the same principle to Uber’s interface. She describes a clearer Uber screen with information such as “Request Uber,” an estimated arrival time, and the home context, contrasting it with a less clear version that lacks an obvious call to action [06:05]. The clearer design helps users immediately understand what is important and what they can do next.

A call to action, such as Uber’s “Confirm pickup,” should be visible at a glance. Rodrigues recommends using sufficient size, contrast, color distinction, and surrounding negative space so the user does not have to search for the primary action [07:02]. The purpose is not merely visual polish; the user should not have to stop and ask what the interface expects them to do.

Consistency is equally important. If one screen says “Confirm pickup” while another uses a different term for the same action, users must work out whether the actions are actually different [07:28]. She extends this problem to code and team boundaries: separate teams may give the same underlying action different names. Shared terminology across the interface, product documentation, and code makes the system easier to understand and maintain.

She also recommends copy that supports scanning: use numerals rather than spelling out numbers when appropriate, put important phrases in bold, keep one idea per paragraph, avoid mixing unrelated points, and use lists or illustrations to highlight structure [08:02]. Large colorful buttons do not automatically make an interface clear. She describes a design in which users had to click repeatedly through nested menus, showing that visual emphasis cannot compensate for a burdensome information structure [08:34].

Users should also retain control over the route they take. A good design should give them enough information to continue, go back, or skip when appropriate, rather than forcing them through a single path [08:34].

Reuse mental models instead of making users relearn

Rodrigues’s next principle is “don’t reinvent things” [09:28]. If an established pattern works and there is no evidence that it is failing, changing it merely to make it novel creates unnecessary learning costs. Interfaces should align with users’ mental models: their existing expectations about how systems, objects, and controls work.

This is closely related to recognition versus recall. Recognition lets users identify a familiar action or symbol when they see it. Recall requires them to remember terminology, sequences, or hidden rules. Rodrigues argues that long acronym lists and lengthy explanations are signs that the system is asking users to recall too much [09:52].

She gives several examples of unnecessary reinvention. One healthcare-related question asks, “Do you or someone you care for have diabetes?” but presents the answers in a way that apparently makes “no” look bad and “yes” look happy. The emotional treatment conflicts with the ordinary meaning of the question: not having diabetes would normally be good news [10:54]. The problem is not the question alone, but the interface’s failure to match the user’s real-world interpretation.

She also describes a navigation or keyboard layout in which familiar number and page-navigation positions were rearranged. She says the design was removed because users found it confusing, and questions whether there was research to justify the change [11:17]. Her standard is not that familiar patterns must never change. A change can be justified when research shows that it solves a real problem; changing a pattern simply because someone wants a different arrangement is not enough.

A similar lesson appears in her example of Facebook changing a familiar feed label. Users disliked the change and groups began asking for the previous terminology to be restored [11:56]. When teams change a familiar system, users may accept the change more readily if they retain a way to return to the previous state. The product team can make a recommendation, but it should not assume it has the right to decide permanently for every user [12:15].

Make navigation and symbols clear

Rodrigues argues that navigation should be effortless, especially for long lists [12:55]. A long list without search forces users to scroll and scan unnecessarily. Where a user can enter a number directly, a drop-down list may add friction instead of reducing it. She also points to tabs and familiar numbering conventions as examples of patterns that remain useful because people already understand them [13:23].

Her phrase “be clear, not clever” summarizes the distinction. A parking-lot display that clearly shows available spaces solves a practical problem without requiring users to interpret an inventive metaphor [13:48]. She also describes a ketchup-container redesign based on feedback that users could not access all of the contents of the previous design. The lesson is to improve the real task rather than adding novelty to the object.

Icons need the same discipline. A magnifying glass might mean search or zoom; a pencil might mean edit or write. The correct interpretation depends on context [14:51]. The familiar save icon based on a floppy disk is technically obsolete, but Rodrigues says it remains useful because users recognize it. Familiarity can therefore be more valuable than literal realism, provided the symbol is widely understood. When an icon is ambiguous, additional context or a label can prevent users from having to recall its meaning.

Design for limitations and provide recovery paths

At [15:55], Rodrigues describes a visually attractive juice machine that has no physical buttons and is controlled through an app. Its failure modes are straightforward: the battery may be empty, the app may crash, a person may not have the app, or the app may not support the device’s version. Someone unfamiliar with the technology may also be unable to operate it.

Her criticism is not that app control is always wrong. It is that the product lacks a simple way for users to recover when the preferred technology fails. A physical button or another basic fallback could allow someone to use the device even when the app, phone, or connection is unavailable [16:44].

She makes a similar point about code-based restaurant ordering. If there is no internet, no battery, or a user is not comfortable with the technology, an ordering process that depends on a code can prevent them from getting food. Restaurants may need a non-digital alternative rather than assuming that every customer can use the preferred digital route [17:06].

This is part of a broader principle: people have limitations, and a design should remain functional under realistic conditions. A minimal or attractive interface is not successful if it stops working for users who lack connectivity, battery, technical familiarity, or an alternative device.

Rodrigues applies the principle to a healthcare system. She contrasts a first screen containing a large amount of undifferentiated information with a more focused screen that surfaces the items most important to the person who logged in, including messages, vaccinations relevant to Brazil, prescriptions, appointments, and alerts [17:44]. The issue is not simply reducing the amount of content. It is prioritizing the content that helps the user act.

Avoid deceptive design and understand the cost of ambiguity

The talk then turns to deceptive design. Rodrigues cites Amazon Prime as an example in which a button or choice was placed inside another control, causing many people to subscribe by mistake [18:44]. Amazon received subscription revenue but later had to pay a large fee and reverse the affected subscriptions. Her point is that an interface can be legally and financially costly when it makes the desired action obvious to the company but not to the user.

She also cites a Citibank incident in which an unclear banking system sent the full loan amount to creditors and the money could not be recovered [19:56]. The excerpt does not establish all of the incident’s details, but Rodrigues uses it to show how unclear system behavior can produce losses far beyond ordinary usability frustration.

She briefly mentions an Apple Maps case involving the removal of a status, but the excerpt does not explain the exact design failure [20:38]. It is therefore only possible to take from that example the general claim that a seemingly small status or interface change can have substantial consequences.

The most serious example is a Jeep Cherokee. Rodrigues describes a situation in which the vehicle’s state was not clear: it was supposed to be locked but was not, and the resulting incident caused the death of an actor [21:02]. She presents the vehicle’s transmission as a mechanical defect and the unclear design of the control as a separate design defect. The example broadens UX beyond websites and apps: any control system that fails to communicate its state can create physical danger.

Make connected systems visible and reduce choice overload

Rodrigues next discusses products that work well together without forcing users to think about how they connect. She uses iPhones and AirPods as an example: people may choose products from the same brand because the connection is reliable, not simply because the products are more fashionable or expensive [22:00]. The interface should make the connection feel automatic while still showing useful status information, such as whether the battery is low and whether the devices are connected [22:24].

This is the principle often expressed as visibility of system status. Users do not need to understand every technical mechanism, but they do need enough information to know what is happening and what they can do when something fails. An error code that communicates nothing to the user is not helpful. Even when security or fraud concerns limit the details that can be shown, the user should generally receive a path toward recovery [29:56].

Rodrigues also describes the “choice paradox.” In a large city with many food and delivery options, a user may scroll for an hour without deciding [22:47]. More options do not automatically create more value; they can create decision paralysis. Her Uber example surfaces three options first and includes a preferred option based on the user’s history, while still allowing the user to explore more choices below [23:20]. The tradeoff is between reducing cognitive load and preserving access to the full range of options.

Progress indicators can help users complete a process. Rodrigues calls attention to the “aha moment,” such as a progress bar or a visible completed step, because users are more likely to continue when they can see that they are making progress [23:50]. She cites an example in which new users who complete a particular task are “10+ more likely” to subscribe to a paid account, though the excerpt does not clarify the precise statistical meaning of “10+.” The reliable design lesson is that clearly marking meaningful progress can encourage completion.

Users should also have ownership of their experience. Allowing them to customize the app and choose which information is presented can make the product feel more relevant than an experience selected entirely by someone else [24:27].

Replace the highest-paid person’s opinion with evidence

Rodrigues explicitly rejects what she calls “I am not a user” decision-making: a director or other senior stakeholder selects a design despite not using the product [24:51]. She refers to this as the highest-paid person’s opinion. Senior input can be useful, but it should not be the only basis for design decisions, especially when the decision-maker is not the person who must use the result.

She uses a Walmart survey to show why apparently positive feedback can be misleading. Walmart asked whether customers wanted the store to be less cluttered. Almost everyone said yes, so the company removed a large amount of inventory and lost money [25:37]. A yes-or-no answer did not reveal what customers considered clutter, what inventory they still valued, or what tradeoffs they would accept. A more useful question would ask what should change in a particular situation and why.

Rodrigues does not reject closed questions entirely. In the Q&A, she says yes-or-no questions are appropriate when the desired information is genuinely binary. The problem arises when a closed question is used to make a broad product decision without collecting context [36:52]. A mixed survey is usually more useful: use quantitative questions for categories such as age, gender, location, usage frequency, or a rating, and use open questions when the team needs to understand why someone is dissatisfied or what should change [38:50].

At Uber, Rodrigues says A/B testing is used extensively, including for buttons, screens, and pop-ups, with experiments run by city [26:30]. This allows teams to compare behavior without immediately rolling out or removing an entire feature. A/B tests can show that a change affects usage; UX research is still needed to understand why users behave that way.

Uber’s geographic scale also supports city- and neighborhood-level analysis. Rodrigues describes trip heat maps and city-specific configurations, which allow the team to identify differences in behavior and test changes in more focused contexts [27:08]. The talk treats data and research as complementary: behavioral evidence reveals where a problem occurs, while interviews and other research help explain the user’s expectations and reasoning.

Account for cultural differences

Rodrigues warns that the same level of explanation or visual density may not work equally well across cultures [27:43]. She describes Americans as more direct and East Asian participants as more likely, in the example presented, to prefer additional detail and context. She places Brazil somewhere in the middle and emphasizes that these are differences in communication style, not a matter of one culture being correct and another being wrong.

She illustrates this with her own work experience. At a German company, she prepared a one-page document containing all the options she had considered, with numbers and supporting detail. At Uber, her director wanted three examples, the context, and a proposal rather than the entire decision history [28:42]. The right amount of information depends on the audience and the communication context.

She also describes an Asian version of a website as more colorful and information-rich, while the American version is cleaner and more direct [29:22]. The design lesson is not to abandon consistency, but to test whether content density, explanation, tone, and presentation match the expectations of the target audience.

AI can assist the interface, but research still needs people

In the Q&A, an audience member asks about AI, voice assistance, dynamically generated screens, personalization, and the possibility that interfaces might change for every user [31:33]. Rodrigues says Uber maintains shared design patterns, including consistent typography and components, across the company [32:51].

Her current distinction is between UI assistance and UX research. AI can help with developer productivity, standardization across applications, comparing interface elements against a shared model, generating text from inputs or personas, and comparing experiment results [33:53]. These are areas where the data and rules are relatively explicit.

She does not believe AI can currently replace the human portion of UX research. For new Uber products involving teenagers, women who prefer women drivers, or future services for children, researchers need to interview people, interpret nuanced expectations, and understand why users feel safe or uncomfortable with particular choices [33:07]. Feelings, expectations, and social context are not as straightforward as structured usage data.

The first AI answer does not fully resolve the audience member’s question about constantly changing, individualized apps or voice-first interactions. The follow-up answer focuses instead on where Uber is currently pushing AI: development effectiveness, shared standards, and generation or comparison tasks, rather than replacing research [34:52].

Key Takeaways

  • A polished interface can still fail if it is based on the team’s preferences rather than users’ observed behavior, expectations, and limitations.
  • People commonly scan instead of reading linearly. Use visual hierarchy, headings, contrast, spacing, alignment, bold emphasis, short paragraphs, and clear grouping to support that behavior.
  • Make the primary action obvious. A visible call to action such as “Confirm pickup” should not compete with secondary controls or require users to search for it.
  • Keep terminology consistent across screens, products, teams, and code. Different names for the same action create unnecessary uncertainty.
  • Prefer recognition over recall. Familiar patterns, conventions, and icons reduce the amount users must memorize, although ambiguous symbols may need labels or context.
  • Do not change established interaction patterns merely to make a design novel. Change them when research demonstrates a real improvement, and provide a way to go back when feasible.
  • Design for failure conditions such as missing connectivity, low battery, unsupported app versions, crashes, and limited technical familiarity. A fallback path is part of the product, not an afterthought.
  • Reduce choice overload by surfacing a small, relevant set of options while preserving access to additional choices. Personalization and history can help, but they should not remove user control.
  • Avoid deceptive choices and nested controls. Ambiguity can cause accidental subscriptions, financial losses, or serious safety problems.
  • Use quantitative data to identify patterns and qualitative research to understand causes. Short, mixed-format surveys are more useful than relying exclusively on yes-or-no questions.
  • A/B testing and city- or neighborhood-level behavioral data can identify whether a change works in a particular context, but human research is still needed to explain the behavior.
  • AI can support development, UI consistency, text generation, and experiment analysis, while interviews and nuanced UX research still require a human in the loop.

Practical Notes

Start with the problem rather than the proposed interface. Define the behavior that is failing, such as users abandoning a page, missing a call to action, selecting the wrong option, or being unable to recover from an error. Then collect evidence through behavioral data, interviews, surveys, usability testing, and experiments. This directly counters the highest-paid-person’s-opinion pattern discussed in the talk.

Run an interface audit focused on scanning. Check whether the most important information is visually prominent, whether related items are grouped, whether spacing and alignment establish a clear order, and whether the main action is visible without additional interpretation. Review copy for long paragraphs, mixed ideas, inconsistent terms, unexplained acronyms, and wording that conflicts with the user’s real-world interpretation.

Create a shared design language for recurring patterns, terminology, colors, typography, components, and common icons. Reuse familiar controls where they already work. If a team proposes changing a familiar pattern, require a clear problem statement and evidence for the change rather than accepting novelty as its justification.

Perform a failure and recovery review for every important flow. Ask what happens if the user has no internet, a dead battery, an outdated app, a crashed app, no account, limited technical familiarity, or an accessibility-related limitation. Provide a manual or alternate route where possible. Show meaningful status information such as connection state or battery state, and do not leave users with an error number that gives them no way to recover.

Reduce choice overload without making decisions permanently on the user’s behalf. A practical pattern from the Uber example is to surface a small number of likely or preferred options first while keeping more options available. For long lists, provide search or direct entry rather than forcing users through a large drop-down or repeated scrolling. Use progress indicators when completing a visible milestone helps users continue.

Use survey formats according to the decision being made. Quantitative questions can capture categories, location, usage frequency, or ratings. Open questions are more valuable when the team needs to know why someone is unhappy or what should change. Keep surveys short, use a mix of closed and open questions, and consider incentives where the product context supports them. A high satisfaction score without a reason may identify sentiment but not an actionable improvement.

Test behavior in the context where the product will be used. Rodrigues describes Uber experiments by city and sometimes by neighborhood because city configurations and user behavior differ. Do not assume that a result from one location or cultural context will transfer unchanged to another. The talk’s examples suggest adapting the amount of context, directness, and visual density while maintaining a coherent product system.

Treat deceptive design as a product risk. Do not hide an important action inside another control, make accidental subscription easier than informed consent, or remove a user’s ability to reverse a consequential decision. The Amazon Prime and Citibank examples show that unclear interaction design can create financial consequences, while the Jeep example shows that the same principle can matter for physical safety.

Use AI for the tasks Rodrigues identifies as relatively structured: improving developer productivity, standardizing UI patterns, generating text from defined inputs or personas, comparing interface implementations, and analyzing experiment results. Keep human researchers involved when the work requires interviews, interpretation of feelings, cultural context, safety expectations, or nuanced reasons behind behavior. The talk does not provide code commands, a programming framework, or a backend architecture; its implementation guidance is at the product, interface, research, and experimentation levels.

The diabetes and healthcare-portal examples concern information architecture and interface interpretation. They are educational examples, not personal medical advice.
